Do Festival Lineups Follow a Network?

Team project for SI 608: Networks, with Lunden Mandigo, Tyrone Pettygrue, and Vaishnavi Venkataraghavan. Full notebooks: GitHub.

Research Questions and Motivation

Our project investigates whether patterns in artist co-performance networks (artists who appear together at music festivals) can help explain or predict future festival lineups. Specifically, we ask:

  • How might a festival-based co-performance network inform which artists perform together at different types of music festivals?
  • If we know the headliner of a music festival, can we predict the rest of the lineup by analyzing network proximity among artists who frequently share stages or events? Can we recommend the headliner?

Network Properties

Density: 0.07304596728805776

These properties indicate a very dense, small-world structure suggesting that most artists are separated by only a few shared events, and clusters of co-performing artists form tightly connected communities.

We found that the Watts-Strogatz model failed to generate a network that produced a result close to our clustering coefficients (WS: 0.75 vs Ours: 0.92) even when p = 0, which suggests our network is more tightly clustered than a regular lattice. Our assortativity coefficient is 0.313, which is indicative of an assortative network (high degree nodes connect to high degree, and vice versa) similar to those of real world physics co-authorship networks.

Clustering a Subgraph to Detect Communities (Modularity and Betweeness)

We randomly sampled about 10% of the network’s total nodes to create graphs that revealed clusters, leveraged clustering methods taught in classes & homeworks (Louvain & Girvan-Newman(first pass)), and found that Louvain was generally better at identifying tight clusters of performers for every subgraph generated. It also has a much larger community structure.

Betweenness isn’t a good clustering technique for our particular problem because, since we have a strong small world network with an average shortest path of about 2, there are multiple alternate paths that could act as a bridge.

Modularity on Top US Festivals (Lollapalooza, Austin City Limits, and Coachella)

Gauging Global Reach of Top 3 US Festivals Based on Modularity Clustering

Artists that featured in these three festivals featured in about 27/30 other festivals, including international ones. After performing modularity clustering on artists that performed at Lollapalooza, Coachella, and Austin City limits (using nodes only from these three festivals), the artist overlap suggests these festivals play a core role in shaping the global touring circuit, bridging different regions and genres. No complete cluster is wholly isolated to one festival, so the clustering is possibly based on other factors like genre and popularity.

A cursory look found that C3 had a considerable number of pop-focused headliners, while C6 had a considerable number of rock/alternative headliners.

There aren’t really any differences in music qualities for each community label. This suggest festival lineups are generally similar when it comes to music diversity.

Every community includes large numbers of EDM style artists. This suggests festivals are choosing EDM artists to perform, and that our data mostly includes EDM artists. A large majority of music festivals are EDM based, while other types of artists perform at smaller niche festivals, a few major conglomerate festivals (i.e. Coachella), or don’t perform at festivals. Music festivals are the main performance space for EDM artists.

Takeaway

Structurally, festival lineups behave like a small-world network: a handful of major festivals (Lollapalooza, Austin City Limits, Coachella) act as hubs connecting otherwise-separate regional and genre-based touring circuits, and that structure alone was enough to predict some of a festival’s future connections. What structure didn’t explain was much about the music itself, communities of co-performing artists didn’t cleanly separate by genre or audio features, which suggests festival booking is driven more by relationships, geography, and industry circuits than by sound alone.
