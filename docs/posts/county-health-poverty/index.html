Health and Poverty Across US Counties

statistics
public-health
SI-618
Does poverty track with health outcomes across U.S. counties and states? Statistical testing and clustering on CDC PLACES and Census ACS data.
Published

November 20, 2024

This project investigates how closely poverty tracks with health outcomes across U.S. counties, and whether that relationship holds up once the analysis moves past a single state to a national, statistically tested comparison. Starting from Michigan and then merging CDC PLACES county-level health data, over 120,000 county-level health records, with Census American Community Survey (ACS) poverty and income estimates across 3,221 counties, this project asks three questions: does poverty rate meaningfully predict chronic-disease prevalence, which health risk behaviors and racial disparities travel alongside that relationship, and do counties and states naturally sort into distinct health-poverty profiles without being told the answer in advance?

Setting a Baseline in Michigan

Before scaling up nationally, the analysis started close to home. Michigan’s county-level median household income clusters between roughly $55,000 and $70,000, with a visible tail at both ends: a handful of counties fall below $50,000, and a few climb above $90,000. That spread previewed the pattern the national analysis would later confirm at scale: income variation across counties is real, and it is not evenly distributed.

Does Poverty Predict Health Outcomes Nationwide?

Moving beyond Michigan, the full CDC PLACES and Census ACS datasets were merged nationally and tested for whether poverty rate meaningfully predicts each health outcome.

Across the full national dataset, four chronic conditions rose consistently with county poverty rate: obesity, high blood pressure, diabetes, and stroke. Of the six outcomes tested, stroke’s relationship to poverty was the steepest and the most statistically pronounced, an analysis of variance across low-, medium-, and high-poverty counties put stroke’s F-statistic at 1,619.59, ahead of coronary heart disease (1,194.28), diabetes (1,176.48), high blood pressure (1,105.12), COPD (1,004.79), and arthritis (693.80). Cancer (non-skin or melanoma) was the outlier: it showed a weak, slightly negative relationship to poverty rather than the positive one every other condition displayed.

A correlation breakdown across the same six outcomes confirmed the pattern: nearly every chronic condition, arthritis, obesity, high cholesterol, high blood pressure, diabetes, correlated positively with poverty rate at a moderate-to-strong level (0.3 to 0.7). Depression correlated more weakly (around 0.2), suggesting mental-health outcomes are shaped by more than income alone. Cancer was the single negative correlation in the set, a pattern that likely says as much about screening and diagnostic access as it does about underlying disease risk: lower-poverty counties may simply catch and report more cancer, not have less of it.

Risk Behaviors and Racial Disparities

The analysis also looked at health risk behaviors (smoking, inactivity, sleep, and similar) and at how the poverty-health relationship differs across racial groups.

Health risk behaviors followed a similarly legible pattern. Smoking correlated strongly with COPD and other respiratory disease, consistent with the well-established biological link. Physical inactivity and short sleep duration also correlated positively with several chronic conditions, suggesting sedentary lifestyles and poor sleep compound the same risk. Binge drinking broke the pattern: its correlations with health outcomes were weak or negative, plausibly because binge drinking is more common in wealthier counties with better healthcare access, though confirming that would require controlling for confounders, like alcohol-specific diseases, that this dataset doesn’t capture.

Breaking the poverty-health relationship out by race surfaced a sharper picture within the Hispanic or Latino population specifically: Mississippi shows consistently worse outcomes than Alaska across arthritis, COPD, obesity, high blood pressure, and depression, along with more tooth loss, a proxy for economic barriers to dental care, and wider variability overall, evidence of deeper within-state health inequality rather than just a higher average. Cancer and stroke rates were the exception, staying similar across both states, in line with the national finding that cancer doesn’t track poverty the way other conditions do.

Clustering Counties Nationally

Correlation shows a relationship exists; clustering tests whether it is strong enough to sort counties into distinct groups without any labels attached. K-Means clustering was run on health outcomes and poverty, evaluated across k = 2 to 15 by silhouette score.

The best fit landed at just two clusters (silhouette = 0.379), the simplest possible split, but not a weak one. Cluster 0, lower-poverty counties, averaged a poverty rate of 11.15%, about 3.2 points below the roughly 14.4% national mean, and ran below average on nearly every outcome: 35.89% obesity, 9.79% diabetes, 29.83% high blood pressure, 12.88% total tooth loss. Cluster 1, higher-poverty counties, averaged 19.20% poverty, nearly 5 points above the mean, and ran above average across the board: 41.26% obesity, 13.18% diabetes, 21.22% tooth loss, 4.05% stroke prevalence. Cancer again broke from the pattern, showing no consistent separation between the two clusters, reinforcing that whatever drives its relationship to poverty isn’t the same mechanism driving every other condition.

The Clearest Pattern: State-Level Clusters

The same clustering approach applied at the state level, rather than the county level, and repeated separately for health outcomes, risk behaviors, and racial breakdowns, produced the cleanest split of the entire analysis. Health-outcome clustering favored two groups (silhouette = 0.379), risk-behavior clustering favored two as well with a stronger fit (silhouette = 0.419), and racial-breakdown clustering favored two groups too, though with the weakest separation of the three (silhouette = 0.309), evidence that race adds less independent structure to the clustering than health outcomes or behavior do once poverty is already accounted for.

A hierarchical dendrogram of health outcomes and poverty made the same finding visually explicit. Hawaii and New Jersey broke off into their own cluster entirely, distinguished by exceptionally low poverty and strong health outcomes. At the other end, Mississippi, Louisiana, West Virginia, and Alabama grouped tightly together, sharing both high poverty and the worst health metrics in the dataset. A larger middle cluster, states like Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Colorado, and Iowa, sat between the two extremes with moderate poverty and comparatively balanced health profiles. A separate dendrogram built on risk behaviors alone reproduced nearly the same split: states like New Mexico, South Carolina, Georgia, and Oklahoma clustered around elevated smoking, inactivity, and poor sleep, while Maryland, Connecticut, New Jersey, and Hawaii clustered around healthier behavior patterns.

Conclusion

Across every level of analysis tested, Michigan’s counties, the full national county dataset, and clustering at both the county and state level, poverty and health outcomes moved together. Poorer counties and states showed consistently worse rates of chronic disease, and unsupervised clustering, run without being told which counties were poor or healthy, converged on the same two groups every time. Hawaii and New Jersey stood out nationally as the healthiest, wealthiest cluster; high-poverty Southern states clustered tightly together on nearly every health metric tested. Cancer was the one consistent exception to an otherwise consistent pattern, a reminder that not every health outcome responds to poverty the same way, and that screening access and diagnostic patterns deserve their own investigation.

Limitations

The CDC PLACES data is cross-sectional, a single year’s snapshot rather than a trend, and several measures are self-reported, which introduces reporting bias that could vary systematically with income or education. County-level aggregation also masks within-county disparities: a county’s average obscures how unevenly poverty and health can be distributed inside its own borders. And because health and poverty are correlated rather than experimentally manipulated, none of this establishes that poverty causes worse health outcomes, only that the two move together closely and consistently enough to be useful for identifying where public-health intervention might matter most.
